Overview

What are MU*s?

MU* (pronounced "moo-star") is the family name for the networked, text-based multiplayer worlds of the pre-graphics internet: real people, in real time, in worlds made entirely of words, reached over a modem and a terminal. For the better part of two decades — before EverQuest, before World of Warcraft — these were the most social games on earth.

The family split in 1989–90 into two broad traditions. MUDs (Multi-User Dungeon / Domain / Dimension) kept the game: combat, levels, quests and treasure. MUSHes (Multi-User Shared Hallucination) took the opposite path: no levelling, no loot — collaborative roleplay and player-built worlds. MUCK, MOO and MUX are siblings from the same branch of the tree.

The family at a glance

MUD — the game worlds

  • Combat, XP, levels, guilds and loot
  • Worlds hand-built by the game's staff
  • Commands: look, north, kill goblin
  • Families: DikuMUD (and Merc/ROM/Circle/SMAUG), LPMud, commercial text games
  • Ancestry: MUD1 (1978) → DikuMUD & LPMud (1989–90)

MUSH — the social worlds

  • Roleplay-first: pose-driven scenes, no levelling
  • Players build the world themselves (@dig, @describe)
  • Commands: pose, page, +finger, @desc me
  • Family: MUSH, MUCK, MOO, MUX — all descended from TinyMUD (1989)
  • Culture: character applications, scene logs, long-lived communities

Both worlds were reached the same way — by telnet — and the codebases even swapped ideas freely. A MUD and a MUSH can run on the same box and speak the same protocol; the difference is culture and design, not plumbing.

Section 1 · Game worlds

MUDs — Multi-User Dungeons

Combat, quests, levelling and loot: the direct ancestors of the modern MMORPG, played entirely in text.

The type of game

A MUD is a shared fantasy (or sci-fi) world described entirely in prose, where the emphasis is on game. You control a character — race, class, statistics — who fights monsters, earns experience points, levels up, joins a guild and pushes into ever-more-dangerous regions. Death is real: you lose your corpse, sometimes your gear, sometimes levels. And you are never alone — hundreds of players shared the same world, adventuring solo or forming groups to raid the dungeons at the deep end of the map.

Movement is by compass direction; everything else is typed. In the dominant Diku family, combat is automatic once you type kill <target> — you keep typing commands (cast, flee, bash) between rounds. The LPMud family leaned more toward quests, skills and a living world with player-run towns. A handful of commercial text games (GemStone, DragonRealms, the Kesmai games) ran on paid services like AOL and CompuServe instead of free telnet.

> look The Market Square of Midgaard. A fountain burbles at the centre of the square. A stout city watchman eyes you. Exits: north, east, south. > say hello You say, "hello" The watchman grunts a greeting. > who Players online: 12 (max 40) > kill goblin You swing your sword at the goblin for 6 damage! The goblin hisses and strikes back for 2 damage.
A typical Diku-family session: move, talk, check who's online, fight.

History & evolution

It all starts with MUD1: Roy Trubshaw's 1978 program at Essex University, expanded by Richard Bartle into the dungeon adventure that named the genre. Through the 1980s, text worlds spread across university networks (AberMUD 1987) and onto commercial services (Islands of Kesmai on CompuServe, GemStone on GEnie).

Then came the codebase explosion. LPMud (1989) and the public-domain DikuMUD (1990) let anyone with a server open a world, and 1991–96 became the golden age — mudlists catalogued 800+ servers, and zMUD (1996) made them genuinely playable with macros and triggers. Graphical MMOs (EverQuest 1999, World of Warcraft 2004) drained the population, but the hobby never died — and hundreds of MUDs are still running today.

Clients & connection methods

Every MUD is a server that speaks the telnet protocol on a TCP port — often an idiosyncratic one like 4000, 4242 or 8888, sometimes 23. To play, you point a client at it and type your name and password. In the 90s that meant dial-up, then a client; today it means the same telnet, a modern client, or often a web browser.

Connect in seconds telnet mume.org 4242 telnet realmsofdespair.com 4000 telnet batmud.bat.org 23
Plain telnetUniversal

Every OS ever shipped one. telnet host port still works against most servers today.

zMUD / CMUD1996 · Windows

Zuggsoft's classic power client: aliases, triggers, macros and maps. Defined "playing MUDs properly".

MUSHclientFree · Windows

Feature-rich client with a powerful scripting language — a longtime favourite for MUDs and MUSHes alike.

MudletModern · open source

Lua scripting, GUI maps, active plugin ecosystem. Still developed and the usual recommendation today.

TinyFugueTerminal

Macros, triggers and status bars inside a plain terminal — beloved by players running in screen or tmux.

Web & mobileNo install

Many running games (Alter Aeon, Aardwolf, play.net) offer in-browser clients and mobile apps.

Extensions matter at the edges: modern servers and clients add MCCP compression, MXP rich text and MSP sound, and web clients bridge via WebSockets — but the core protocol is unchanged since 1983.

Section 2 · Social worlds

MUSHes — Multi-User Shared Hallucinations

Roleplay, worldbuilding and community: collaborative fiction with no levelling, no loot, and worlds built by their players.

The type of game

A MUSH is a text world built around roleplay and community rather than game. There is usually no experience, no levelling and little or no coded combat — the point is collaborative interactive fiction. Players create characters, describe themselves (@desc me), and act scenes together using pose for actions and say for speech. The setting is often a fandom — Pern, Middle-earth, Amber, Star Wars, Star Trek, the World of Darkness — or an original world.

The world itself is player-built: with the @dig and @describe commands, players construct rooms, objects and entire towns, while the game's staff (wizards, gods) hold the keys to the master design. The culture is famously literary — most MUSHes ask you to submit a character application before you play, and long, carefully-written scene logs are the medium of the art form. Communities were small, intense and astonishingly durable: many ran for a decade or more, and some are still going after 25+ years.

> pose warms their hands by the hearth and smiles at you. You see Lyra warms their hands by the hearth and smiles at you. > say welcome back to the Weyr! You say, "welcome back to the Weyr!" > page Lyra = any news? Lyra pages: "Join us at +job 42 — we're plotting a hatching scene :D" > +finger Lyra Lyra — joined 2009 · Dragonrider (Bronze) · alts: none
A typical MUSH session: poses, dialogue, private pages, and community commands.

History & evolution

Everything traces to TinyMUD (1989): Jim Aspnes stripped the combat out of the MUD and gave players the power to build the world with @dig and @describe — a radical idea that launched an entire family. Forks came within a year: TinyMUCK (1990), MOO (1990, Xerox PARC), and TinyMUSH (1991), whose name — Multi-User Shared Hallucination — stuck as the label for the whole branch.

The 1990s were the golden age: the 1996 Almost-Complete List of MUSHes catalogued 76 of them, from Pern to Middle-earth to Amber, and the biggest (Elendor, AmberMUSH) ran for decades. As graphical MMOs rose, MUSH communities proved fiercely loyal and simply kept going. In 2011 the AresMUSH engine rebuilt the format for the web — browser play, integrated chat and character sheets — and sparked a new generation of games.

Clients & connection methods

MUSHes use the same telnet plumbing as MUDs — a server, a port, and any telnet-capable client. Historically the MUSH community favoured TinyFugue (a terminal client with custom display bars) and MUSHclient; modern players use Mudlet, macOS's Textual, or — increasingly — nothing at all, because AresMUSH games are played entirely in the browser.

Connect in seconds telnet lambda.moo.mud.org 8888 telnet furry.org 8888 Wilds of Pern — play in your browser
Plain telnetUniversal

Most MUSHes are still reachable with a raw telnet client — no features, no frills, total authenticity.

TinyFugueTerminal classic

The MUSH community's traditional client: macros, triggers, custom bars, and perfect for poses and pages.

MUSHclientFree · Windows

Lua-scriptable client that has served MUSHers for decades, with built-in support for logging scenes.

Mudlet / TextualModern · cross-platform

Mudlet for scripting and maps on any OS; Textual for a clean native macOS MUD/MUSH session.

AresMUSH web portalsBrowser only

Modern games ship their own web portal — chat, scenes, character sheets and wiki, all in the browser.

A MUSH-specific ritual to know before you connect: many games gate full access behind a character application (+app) — you introduce the character you want to play and a staff member approves it. Browsing around and chatting in the common room is usually still open to guests.
